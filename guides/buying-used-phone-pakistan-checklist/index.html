Buying a Used Phone in Pakistan: The Complete Checklist

A used phone is a judgement call made under time pressure, usually in a noisy shop with the seller watching. The way to make it a good one is to decide your checks in advance and run them in order. This checklist is split into what to do before you leave home, what to do at the counter, and how to judge whether the price is worth the risk at all.

Stage one: before you go

Ask the seller for the IMEI in advance and send it by SMS to 8484. That free DIRBS check tells you whether the device is PTA approved before you have travelled anywhere. On a dual-SIM phone, insist on both IMEIs and check both — approval of one says nothing about the other. Our PTA approval guide covers what the replies mean.

Next, find out what the phone costs new. That number is your ceiling, and knowing it changes the whole negotiation. Browse our best phones under Rs 50,000 guide for the current market, and look at the specific model — the Redmi Note 14 4G, for instance, sells new at Rs 57,999 officially. Walking in without that figure means accepting the seller's version of it.

Stage two: in the shop, minute by minute

  1. Dial *#06# and match the IMEI on screen against the sticker on the box. A mismatch ends the conversation.
  2. Make a real call. Listen through the earpiece, speak so the other side can judge the microphone, and watch that the phone holds the network.
  3. Open both cameras and take test shots. Fire the flash. Check that nothing is fogged, scratched or stuck.
  4. Try the fingerprint sensor with your own finger, more than once.
  5. Display a plain white background, then a plain grey one, and look for burn-in, patches of discoloration or uneven brightness. Replaced screens often give themselves away here with washed-out colours, and some phones show unusually thick bezels around a non-original panel.
  6. Play a video for a few minutes and watch the battery percentage. A healthy battery falls gradually; a dying one drops in sudden steps or loses several percent almost immediately.
  7. Plug in a charger and gently check the port. Noticeable wobble in the charging port means wear you will be paying to repair.
  8. Finally, watch the seller remove their Google, Samsung or iCloud account and factory-reset the phone in front of you. Never accept a promise that it will be done later.
An account-locked phone can become useless. If the previous owner's account is still signed in, FRP on Android or Activation Lock on an iPhone can lock you out after a reset — and the seller may be impossible to reach by then.

Stage three: the paperwork

Ask for the original box, and check its IMEI sticker against the phone itself. Take the warranty card too if any warranty remains — that remaining cover is part of what you are buying. For an expensive phone, get a written receipt from the shop rather than relying on a verbal understanding. It costs the seller a minute and gives you something to point to if the device turns out to be blocked or faulty within days.

Stage four: does the price make sense?

Now compare the asking price with the new price you looked up earlier, and be unsentimental about it.

Our advice, stated plainly as advice rather than a rule: if a used phone is priced within roughly 10–15% of its new price, the new phone is usually the smarter buy. You gain a full warranty, a battery at the start of its life, and no doubt about the phone's history — and on a budget model that package is often worth more than the small saving. Used phones earn their place when the discount is large enough to compensate for everything you cannot verify. If it is not, walk to the new counter instead.
